About the Text Similarity Checker

The text similarity checker compares two passages and scores how alike they are, combining unique-word overlap with two-word phrase overlap so that reordered or lightly edited copies still score high. It is a practical duplicate-content check for writers and site owners.

How to use the Text Similarity Checker — step by step

  1. Paste the first text into Text A.
  2. Paste the second into Text B.
  3. Read the overall similarity and the word/phrase breakdown.
  4. Above roughly 50%, rewrite one version properly rather than spinning words.

Worked example

Two product descriptions sharing most phrases score around 70–80% even with sentences swapped — the bigram check catches the shared phrases that a simple word count would miss.

When to use it

Use it to check a freelancer's 'unique' content, compare your page with a competitor's, or audit location pages that were templated from one original.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming a low score means original ideas — it measures wording, not thought.
  • Publishing location pages that differ only in the city name; similarity tools and Google both see through it.
  • Checking tiny texts — under about 30 words, scores swing wildly.

Using similarity checks as a real content audit

Run this check in three places where duplication quietly costs you. Your own pages against each other: service pages for different towns, or product pages sharing one description, compete with each other in search results — Google picks one and ignores the rest, a problem called cannibalisation. Freelance deliveries: compare a submitted article against the top-ranking page on the topic; anything above roughly 50% needs a structural rewrite — new examples, new order, new angle — not synonym swaps. Before a site migration: check old and new versions of key pages so you do not accidentally publish both. When you rewrite, change the substance: different worked numbers, different use cases, a different reader. Two texts can share almost no wording and still be the same article — and the reverse, sharing facts but written independently, is exactly what original coverage looks like.

Text Similarity Checker — frequently asked questions

What similarity is acceptable?

Under about 25% is normal overlap for two texts on the same topic. Boilerplate like addresses inflates scores legitimately.

Is this how Google detects duplicates?

Google uses far more sophisticated fingerprinting, but the principle — shared phrases at scale — is the same.

Can this check a student's essay for plagiarism?

It can flag overlap against one specific source you paste in, but it is not a plagiarism database — it does not search the web or academic libraries. Treat a high score as a prompt to look closer, not a verdict.
