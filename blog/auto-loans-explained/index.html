Auto Loans Explained: Terms, Rates and Total Cost

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

Auto loans look simple: borrow money, buy the car, pay monthly. But two loans with the same monthly payment can differ in cost by thousands, because the term and the interest rate quietly decide what the car really costs you. This guide explains how auto loans work so you can judge any offer on its total cost — not just its monthly figure.

How auto loans work

An auto loan is a secured loan: the car itself is the security, which is why rates are usually lower than credit cards or unsecured personal loans. You borrow a set amount — the car price minus your deposit and any trade-in — and repay it in equal monthly instalments over a fixed term, commonly 36 to 72 months. Each payment is part interest, part principal, with interest front-loaded in the early months.

Auto loan terms: why longer is not cheaper

Stretching the term lowers the monthly payment, which is exactly why dealers offer 72- and even 84-month loans. But the total interest climbs steeply:

Example: borrowing $28,000 at 7.5% — over 48 months the payment is about $677 and total interest is roughly $4,500. Over 72 months the payment drops to about $484, but total interest rises to roughly $6,900. The “cheaper” loan costs about $2,400 more.

There is a second risk: a long loan can leave you owing more than the car is worth for years, because cars lose value fastest early on. Compare any offer properly with the Auto Loan Calculator — enter price, deposit, rate and term and read the total interest line before anything else.

What decides your auto loan rate

  • Credit score — the single biggest factor; top scores get rates several points lower.
  • Term length — shorter terms usually carry lower rates.
  • Deposit size — borrowing less of the car's value reduces the lender's risk.
  • New vs used — used-car loans typically cost 1–3 points more.
  • Where you borrow — banks and credit unions often beat dealer finance; dealer deals sometimes win when manufacturers subsidise the rate.

Get the loan before you visit the dealer

Arranging a pre-approval from your own bank first gives you a rate to beat and turns the dealer conversation into a simple question: “Can you do better than this?” It also keeps the focus on the car's price instead of a monthly payment that can hide a long term and add-ons.

The true cost of the car

The loan is only part of ownership. Add insurance (which our car insurance guide breaks down), fuel or charging, maintenance and depreciation. A car that fits your budget on the loan alone can still strain it once running costs are included.

Key takeaways

  • Judge auto loans on total cost: amount borrowed + total interest + fees.
  • Longer terms cut the payment but raise total interest and the risk of owing more than the car is worth.
  • A pre-approved rate from your bank is your best negotiating tool.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good term for an auto loan?

For most buyers, 48–60 months balances payment size against total interest. Longer terms make sense only if the rate is genuinely low and you keep the car for many years.

How much deposit should I put down?

Around 20% on a new car and 10% on a used car is a common guideline — it reduces interest and protects you against early depreciation.

Can I pay an auto loan off early?

Usually yes, and it saves interest — but check for early-repayment fees in your agreement first.

Does the monthly payment tell me if a deal is good?

No. The same payment can hide very different terms and rates. Always ask for the total amount repayable.
