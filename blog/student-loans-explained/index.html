Student Loans: Repayment Plans and Interest, in Plain English

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

Student loans are often the first serious debt a person takes on — and the least explained. The statements mix balances, interest and plan names in ways that make it hard to answer a simple question: what will I actually repay? This guide explains student loans in plain English: how the interest works, what repayment plans do, and how to think about extra payments.

How student loan interest builds

Interest usually starts accruing from the day each part of the loan is paid out — including while you are still studying. That means you can graduate owing more than you borrowed, even before your first payment. The interest rate may be fixed for the life of the loan or linked to inflation or a market rate, depending on the system and when you borrowed. Either way, the mechanics are the same: interest is charged on the outstanding balance, so the balance falls slowly at first and faster later — exactly like the loans modelled in our Student Loan Calculator.

Student loan repayment plans, decoded

  • Standard / fixed-term plans — a set monthly payment clears the loan in a fixed number of years. Predictable, and usually the cheapest in total interest.
  • Income-based plans — payments are a percentage of your income above a threshold, so they rise and fall with your earnings. Common in the UK (where repayment is collected through payroll) and available in the US through income-driven plans. Lower earners pay less each month but the loan lasts longer.
  • Graduated plans — payments start low and rise on a schedule, betting that your income will grow.
  • Write-off / forgiveness — some systems cancel the remaining balance after a set number of years or for certain public-service careers. A large share of borrowers on income-based plans will never repay in full — which changes the maths of extra payments completely.

Should you pay extra towards student loans?

It depends on your plan. On a standard fixed-term loan, extra payments reduce interest exactly as they do on any loan — run the numbers in the Loan Calculator. On an income-based plan with a realistic write-off, extra payments may simply reduce the amount that would have been cancelled — in that case the same money often works harder in an emergency fund or pension. There is no universal answer; there is only your plan's answer.

Order your debts by interest rate

Student loans usually carry lower rates than credit cards. If you have both, the expensive debt almost always deserves the spare money first — see how payoff order works in our snowball vs avalanche guide.

Key takeaways

  • Interest typically accrues from day one, including while you study.
  • Income-based plans tie payments to earnings and may end in a write-off.
  • Whether extra payments help depends entirely on your plan type — check before paying.

Frequently asked questions

Do student loans affect my credit score?

In the US, yes — they appear on your credit file and payment history matters. In the UK, student loans do not appear on credit files, though lenders may consider the payments when assessing affordability for a mortgage.

What happens if I move abroad?

Most systems still require repayment, with thresholds adjusted by country. Tell your loan provider — ignoring it can trigger penalties.

Can student loans be refinanced?

Private refinancing exists in the US and can lower the rate for high earners with strong credit, but it gives up the protections of federal loans. Weigh that trade-off carefully.

Is it worth clearing a student loan early?

On a standard plan, early repayment saves real interest. On an income-based plan with likely write-off, often not — model your own case first.
