How Search Engines Work, in Plain English

Understanding how search engines work turns SEO from superstition into common sense. Behind the search box, Google and Bing run a three-stage system: they crawl the web to discover pages, index what they find into a giant library, and rank results for each search. Every practical SEO task you will ever do supports one of these three stages.

Stage 1: Crawling — how search engines find pages

Automated programs called crawlers (or spiders) follow links across the web, downloading pages as they go. They start from pages they already know and follow every link they find — which is why internal links between your own pages matter, and why a page nobody links to may never be discovered. Files like robots.txt tell crawlers where they may and may not go, and a sitemap.xml hands them a tidy list of your pages. Both are easy to create: try our Robots.txt Generator and Sitemap Generator.

Stage 2: Indexing — the giant library

Crawled pages are analysed — text, headings, images, language, location signals — and stored in the index, a database of hundreds of billions of pages. Not everything crawled gets indexed: duplicates, thin pages and pages marked “noindex” are left out. If a page is not in the index, it cannot appear in results at all, which is why “why isn't my page on Google?” is usually an indexing question, not a ranking one.

Stage 3: Ranking — how search engines order results

When you search, the engine scans its index for relevant pages and orders them using hundreds of signals, including:

  • Relevance — how well the page's words, headings and topic match the search's intent.
  • Quality and trust — original, accurate content; links from other reputable sites act as votes of confidence.
  • Usability — mobile-friendliness, speed and a page that answers the query without tricks.
  • Context — your location, language and search history personalise the order.

This is why tools like our On-Page SEO Checker focus on titles, headings and content quality — they are the relevance signals you control directly. Checking that your main topic appears naturally in your text (with a keyword density check) is part of the same stage.

What this means for your site

Make pages easy to crawl (clear links, a sitemap), worth indexing (original, substantial content) and easy to rank (honest titles, helpful structure, other sites happy to link to you). Our meta tags guide covers the ranking signals on the page itself.

Key takeaways

  • Crawling discovers pages, indexing stores them, ranking orders them.
  • No index, no ranking — discovery and quality come before everything else.
  • SEO is simply helping each of the three stages do its job.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take for a new page to appear on Google?

Anywhere from hours to weeks. Submitting your sitemap in Google Search Console and linking the page from your existing pages speeds discovery up.

Why is my page crawled but not indexed?

Common causes: thin or duplicated content, a noindex tag, or the page being too similar to another. Improve the content's usefulness and uniqueness first.

Do paid ads affect organic rankings?

No. Advertising and organic ranking are separate systems; paying for ads does not raise your natural results.

How often do search engines re-crawl pages?

Popular, frequently updated pages may be revisited daily; quiet pages, every few weeks or months. Fresh, useful updates encourage more frequent visits.
