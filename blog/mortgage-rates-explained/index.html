Mortgage Rates Explained: How Your Rate Changes Your Monthly Payment

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

When people shop for a home loan, mortgage rates get most of the attention — and for good reason. Your rate decides your monthly payment, how much of each payment is interest, and the total cost of the loan over 15, 20 or 30 years. Yet many buyers compare rates without seeing what a difference of half a percent actually means in money. This guide fixes that with real numbers.

What mortgage rates actually are

A mortgage rate is the price you pay for borrowing, expressed as a yearly percentage of the loan balance. If you borrow $360,000 at 6.5%, the lender charges roughly 6.5% of the remaining balance in interest each year, collected inside your monthly payment. Early in the loan the balance is high, so most of each payment is interest. As the balance falls, more of each payment clears the loan itself.

Rates are not random. Lenders set them from wider market rates, then adjust for your credit score, deposit size, loan term and the type of property. Two neighbours can be offered noticeably different mortgage rates on the same day.

How mortgage rates change your monthly payment

Take a $360,000 loan over 30 years and change only the rate:

  • At 5.5% — about $2,044 a month, and roughly $376,000 of interest over the term.
  • At 6.5% — about $2,275 a month, and roughly $459,000 of interest.
  • At 7.5% — about $2,517 a month, and roughly $546,000 of interest.

One percentage point — from 6.5% to 5.5% — saves about $231 every month and around $83,000 across the loan. That is why chasing a slightly better rate, or improving your credit score before you apply, can be worth more than negotiating a small discount on the house price. Why the term matters as much as the rate

A shorter term usually comes with a lower rate and far less total interest, but a higher monthly payment. A 15-year loan at 6% on the same $360,000 costs about $3,038 a month — yet only about $187,000 of interest in total. The right choice depends on whether your budget can carry the higher payment safely.

Fixed vs adjustable mortgage rates

A fixed rate stays the same for the period agreed — sometimes the whole loan, sometimes the first 2, 3 or 5 years. An adjustable rate starts lower but moves with the market after an initial period, so your payment can rise. Adjustable rates can suit someone who will move or refinance soon, but they carry real risk if rates climb. Always check the worst-case payment, not just the starting one.

How to get a better mortgage rate

  • Improve your credit score in the months before applying — pay bills on time and clear small balances.
  • Save a bigger deposit — borrowing a smaller share of the price usually unlocks better rates.
  • Compare at least three lenders — the first quote is rarely the best.
  • Watch the fees — a low rate with high arrangement fees can cost more overall. Compare the total cost, which our Loan Calculator helps you sense-check.

Key takeaways

  • Mortgage rates affect both your monthly payment and, even more, your total interest.
  • A 1% lower rate on a typical loan can save tens of thousands over the term.
  • Term length, deposit size and credit score all move the rate you are offered.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good mortgage rate?

There is no single number — it depends on current market rates, your credit profile and the loan type. The practical test is comparing quotes from several lenders on the same day for the same loan.

How much does 1% change a mortgage payment?

On a $360,000, 30-year loan, each 1% is roughly $230–$240 a month — and around $85,000 of interest over the full term.

Should I choose a 15-year or 30-year mortgage?

A 15-year loan saves a large amount of interest but demands a much higher monthly payment. Choose the shorter term only if the payment still leaves room for savings and emergencies.

Can I change my rate later?

Yes — that is what refinancing does. Our guide to refinancing a mortgage explains when it genuinely saves money.
