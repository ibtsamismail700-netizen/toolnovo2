Credit Card Debt: The Snowball vs Avalanche Payoff, Explained

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

If you carry credit card debt on more than one card, the hardest question is not how much to pay — it is which card to pay first. Two well-tested strategies answer it: the snowball method and the avalanche method. Both work. They differ in what they optimise — your motivation, or your money.

First, the rule both methods share

Whichever method you pick, you pay the minimum on every card, on time, every month. Missed minimums trigger fees and damage your credit score, and no strategy survives that. Then every spare dollar goes to one target card until it is gone, and its payment rolls into the next card. That rolling payment is where both methods get their power.

The snowball method for credit card debt

With the snowball, you attack the smallest balance first, regardless of interest rate. Clearing a card completely — even a small one — gives a quick, visible win. That win keeps people going, and a plan you stick to beats a perfect plan you abandon in month three.

Example: you owe $400 on Card A, $1,800 on Card B and $3,500 on Card C. Snowball order: A, then B, then C. Card A could be gone within a couple of months, freeing its payment to join the attack on Card B.

The avalanche method for credit card debt

With the avalanche, you attack the highest interest rate first. This is the mathematically cheapest route: every dollar of high-rate balance you remove stops the most expensive interest from growing. On larger debts held for a long time, the avalanche can save hundreds compared with the snowball.

So which one should you choose?

If the interest difference between the two orders is small, choose the snowball — motivation is worth more than a few dollars. If one card charges a much higher rate than the others and the balances are large, the avalanche saving is real money; choose it, and set small milestones to keep yourself motivated. Many people blend them: clear one tiny balance for momentum, then switch to highest-rate order.

What actually clears credit card debt fastest

  • Stop adding to the cards while you repay — move daily spending to a debit card or cash.
  • Raise the payment, even slightly — an extra $25–$50 a month shortens the plan by months. Check the effect in the Credit Card Payoff Calculator.
  • Automate the payments so a busy month never becomes a missed month.
  • Throw windfalls at the target card — refunds, bonuses and sold items all count.

Key takeaways

  • Snowball = smallest balance first, best for motivation. Avalanche = highest rate first, cheapest overall.
  • Both require minimum payments on every card, every month.
  • The best method is the one you will still be following in six months.

Frequently asked questions

Which method saves more money?

The avalanche, because it removes the most expensive interest first. The saving grows with bigger balances and bigger rate differences.

Should I close cards as I clear them?

Not automatically. Closing old cards can shorten your credit history and raise your credit utilisation, which can lower your score. Many people keep cleared cards open with no balance.

What if I can only afford minimum payments?

Pay them on time to protect your credit, and look for any small amount to add to one card. If payments are genuinely unaffordable, speak to your card provider or a free debt-advice charity — they can often freeze interest.

Does the order really matter that much?

Less than paying consistently. The difference between methods is usually far smaller than the difference between having a plan and not having one.
