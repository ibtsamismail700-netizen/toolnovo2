Break-Even Point Explained for New Businesses

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

The break-even point is the level of sales at which your business covers all its costs — below it you lose money, above it you make profit. It converts a vague hope (“sell enough”) into a precise target (“sell 214 units a month”), which is why lenders, landlords and sensible owners all ask for it. This guide explains the break-even point with a worked example you can adapt in minutes.

The three ingredients of the break-even point

  • Fixed costs — costs that stay the same whatever you sell: rent, insurance, software, salaries, loan payments. Add up a month's worth.
  • Variable cost per unit — what each sale costs you: materials, packaging, shipping, platform fees.
  • Price per unit — what the customer pays.

The difference between price and variable cost is called the contribution — the amount each sale contributes towards covering fixed costs. Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.

Example: fixed costs are $3,000 a month. You sell a product for $50; each unit costs $22 in materials, packaging and fees. Contribution = $28. Break-even = $3,000 ÷ $28 ≈ 108 units a month (about $5,400 of sales). Unit 109 is where profit starts.

Using your break-even point

  • Pricing decisions — a lower price needs many more sales to break even; the calculator shows exactly how many (pricing method in our product pricing guide).
  • Cost control — every fixed cost you cut lowers the target permanently.
  • Reality checks — if break-even needs 108 sales a month and your market realistically offers 60, the plan — not the effort — needs changing.
  • Loan readiness — lenders ask because it proves you understand your business (see how business loans work).

Break-even for services

Service businesses break even in hours instead of units: fixed costs ÷ (hourly rate − variable cost per hour, such as subcontractors or travel). The logic is identical — and it is also how honest freelance day rates are built.

Key takeaways

  • Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit).
  • Know the number monthly, and recheck it whenever prices or costs change.
  • If the target is unreachable, change the model — price, costs or product — not just the effort.

Frequently asked questions

Is break-even the same as profit?

No — break-even is zero profit, zero loss. It is the threshold profit starts beyond, which is exactly why it matters.

Should my salary count as a fixed cost?

Yes, if the business must pay you to survive. Excluding your own pay flatters the number and hides the real target.

What if I sell several products?

Use a weighted-average contribution based on your typical sales mix, or calculate break-even in revenue terms: fixed costs ÷ overall margin percentage.

How often should I recalculate?

Whenever rent, prices, supplier costs or fees change — and at least quarterly while the business is young.
