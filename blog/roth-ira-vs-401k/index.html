Roth IRA vs 401(k): Which One Builds More Wealth in 2026?

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

Two accounts dominate retirement saving in the United States: the 401(k), offered through an employer, and the Roth IRA, which you open yourself. Both shelter your investments from yearly taxes. The real difference is when you pay tax — and that single difference can be worth tens of thousands of dollars by the time you retire.

This guide compares them using the official IRS limits for 2026, explains the employer match rule that should drive your first decision, and walks through a worked example so you can see the numbers for yourself.

The Quick Answer

If your employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute enough to get the full match first — that is an instant 100% return no account choice can beat. After that, most people benefit from funding a Roth IRA next (tax-free withdrawals in retirement, far more investment choice), and then returning to the 401(k) if they can save more. The detailed reasoning is below.

How Each Account Is Taxed

Traditional 401(k): tax break now, tax bill later. Contributions come out of your paycheck before tax, so they lower your taxable income today. The money grows tax-deferred, and you pay ordinary income tax on everything you withdraw in retirement.

Roth IRA: tax bill now, tax-free later. You contribute money you have already paid tax on, so there is no deduction today. In exchange, qualified withdrawals in retirement — including decades of investment growth — come out completely tax-free.

A useful way to think about it: a traditional 401(k) taxes the harvest, a Roth IRA taxes the seed. If your tax rate will be higher later, the Roth usually wins. If it will be lower later, the traditional account usually wins. Many workers are in a lower bracket early in their careers, which is why the Roth IRA is so popular with younger savers.

Contribution Limits for 2026

The IRS raised both limits for 2026 (Notice 2025-67, announced November 13, 2025):

The 401(k) clearly wins on capacity — you can shelter more than three times as much per year. The Roth IRA wins on flexibility, which we will get to below.

The Employer Match Changes Everything

Many employers match part of what you put into a 401(k) — a common formula is 50% or 100% of the first 3–6% of your salary. On a $60,000 salary with a 100% match on the first 3%, contributing $1,800 earns you another $1,800 from your employer. That is an immediate doubling of your money before any market growth.

No feature of the Roth IRA can compete with that. Whatever you decide about the rest of your savings, never leave a match unclaimed.

A Worked Example: $6,000 a Year From Age 30

Suppose you invest $6,000 a year from age 30 to 65 — 35 years — and earn a hypothetical 8% average annual return. (This is an illustration, not a prediction; markets do not move in straight lines.)

  • Total you pay in: $210,000
  • Value at 65: about $1,034,000
  • Growth: about $824,000

Now the tax difference. In a Roth IRA, that roughly $1,034,000 is yours to withdraw tax-free — every dollar of the $824,000 growth included. In a traditional 401(k), the same balance is taxed as income when you withdraw it; at a flat 22% rate, tax would take about $227,000, leaving roughly $807,000. The 401(k) saver did get tax relief on the way in — $6,000 a year contributed pre-tax costs only about $4,680 of take-home pay at a 22% rate — which is why financial planners compare the two using your tax rate today versus your expected rate in retirement.

One more option worth knowing: many employers now offer a Roth 401(k), which combines the high 401(k) limit with Roth tax treatment. If your plan offers it and you expect higher taxes later, it can be the best of both.

Who Should Prioritise Which?

Early career / lower tax bracket now: the Roth IRA usually wins. You pay tax at today's low rate and never again.

Peak earning years / high bracket now: the traditional 401(k)'s upfront deduction is valuable, especially if you expect a lower income — and lower bracket — in retirement.

No workplace plan (freelancers, gig workers): the Roth IRA is your main account, up to the $7,500 limit for 2026.

High savers: use both. The accounts are complements, not rivals — a common order is: 401(k) up to the full match → Roth IRA up to $7,500 → back to the 401(k) up to $24,500.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have a 401(k) and a Roth IRA at the same time?
Yes. The limits are separate, and having a workplace plan does not stop you contributing to a Roth IRA (subject to the income phase-out above).

What if I earn too much for a Roth IRA?
Above the phase-out range you cannot contribute directly. Some people use a "backdoor Roth" conversion instead — a legitimate strategy, but one with tax traps, so research it carefully or ask a tax professional first.

Is the employer match counted in my $24,500 limit?
No. The $24,500 limit for 2026 covers only your own contributions. Employer money sits under the separate combined cap of $72,000.

Which account should a freelancer choose?
With no employer plan, start with a Roth IRA. Self-employed readers may also qualify for a Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA with much higher limits.

The Bottom Line

The 401(k) versus Roth IRA debate is really a question about timing your taxes — plus one rule that overrides everything: always capture the full employer match first. Beyond that, younger and lower-bracket savers generally get more from the Roth IRA's tax-free future, while high earners often prefer the 401(k)'s deduction today. If you can fund both, you get tax diversification as well — and options are valuable in retirement.
