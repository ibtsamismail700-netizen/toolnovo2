Car Insurance: What Actually Affects Your Premium

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

Your car insurance premium can feel arbitrary: it jumps at renewal, differs between neighbours, and rarely matches the price in the advert. In reality insurers price risk from a fairly predictable list of factors. Understanding your car insurance premium means understanding that list — and which parts of it you can legally influence.

The car itself drives your car insurance premium

Insurers group cars by repair cost, parts prices, theft rates and performance. A small, common hatchback with cheap parts sits in a low group; a powerful or rare car with expensive panels sits in a high one. The car's value matters too, but repair cost matters more — a modest car with pricey technology in its bumpers can still be expensive to insure. When you are choosing a car, an insurance quote before you buy is worth as much as the Auto Loan Calculator is for the loan.

You: age, history and where the car sleeps

  • Driving history — claims and convictions raise premiums for years; a clean record is the strongest discount there is.
  • Experience — younger and newer drivers pay far more, because accident statistics say they claim more.
  • Address and parking — local theft and accident rates, and whether the car is garaged, on a drive or on the street.
  • Mileage and use — more miles means more exposure; commuting and business use cost more than social use only.
  • Who else drives — adding a young driver raises the price; sometimes adding an experienced second driver lowers it.

The cover you choose

Comprehensive cover costs more than third-party only — though not always; sometimes comprehensive is cheaper because of who buys each type. Your voluntary excess (the amount you pay towards a claim) trades directly against the premium: a higher excess lowers the price but raises your cost if you claim. Optional extras — breakdown cover, courtesy car, legal protection, windscreen cover — each add a little, and they add up.

Legal ways to lower your premium

  • Compare at every renewal — loyalty is rarely rewarded; new-customer prices are usually keener.
  • Pay annually if you can — monthly instalments usually include an interest charge.
  • Set mileage honestly but accurately — overestimating miles overcharges you; underestimating can void cover.
  • Improve security — approved alarms, immobilisers and garaging can help.
  • Build no-claims years and consider protecting them once you have several.

Never be tempted to misstate facts — a wrong job title, address or main driver (“fronting”) can void the policy exactly when you need it. Keep an emergency fund so a higher excess is a real option, not a gamble.

Key takeaways

  • Your premium is priced on the car, your history, your mileage and your cover choices.
  • The biggest savings come from comparing at renewal and keeping a clean record.
  • Only ever lower a premium with true information — false details can void cover.

Frequently asked questions

Why did my premium go up when nothing changed?

Insurers reprice whole risk pools — repair costs, theft trends and claims in your area all feed in, even if your own record is spotless. It is the strongest reason to compare quotes every year.

Does a higher excess always save money?

It lowers the premium, but you must be able to pay that excess after an accident. Choose the highest excess you could genuinely afford.

Is third-party insurance always cheapest?

No. Comprehensive cover is sometimes cheaper because insurers price by the risk profile of typical buyers. Always compare both.

Can my job title change my premium?

Yes, insurers use occupation in their risk models. Describe your job truthfully — small honest differences in wording are fine, inventing a different job is not.
