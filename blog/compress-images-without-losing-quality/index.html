How to Compress Images Without Losing Quality

Learning to compress images properly is one of the highest-value skills on the web: smaller images mean faster pages, cheaper storage and emails that actually send. The good news is that most photos can lose 70–90% of their file size with no visible change. The trick is doing the steps in the right order.

Step 1: Resize before you compress images

The biggest wins come from dimensions, not quality sliders. A photo straight from a phone may be 4000 pixels wide, but a blog shows it at 800. Resizing to the actual display size can cut the file by 80% before any compression happens. Our Image Resizer handles this in seconds — set the width the image will really be shown at, and keep the aspect ratio locked.

Step 2: Choose the right format

Format decides how much compression is possible. Photographs belong in JPEG or WebP; graphics with sharp edges and text belong in PNG or WebP; WebP usually beats both on size at the same quality. If you are unsure which to pick, our JPG vs PNG vs WebP guide compares them with examples. Saving a photo as PNG is the single most common cause of mysteriously huge image files.

Step 3: Compress images at the right quality

For JPEG and WebP, a quality setting around 75–85% is the sweet spot: the file shrinks dramatically while the human eye sees no difference at normal viewing sizes. Go lower for thumbnails, higher only for images people will zoom into. Then zoom to 100% and check faces, text and gradients — if they look clean, you are done. Our Image Compressor shows before-and-after sizes and previews, runs entirely in your browser, and never uploads your files.

A practical workflow

  • Keep the original file untouched as your master copy.
  • Resize to the display size (for example 1600px wide for a hero image, 800px for in-article images — roughly double the display size for sharp screens).
  • Export as WebP where supported, JPEG as the universal fallback.
  • Compress at 80% quality, check at 100% zoom, adjust once if needed.

Why it matters

Images are usually the heaviest part of a web page. Compressing them is the fastest single fix in our guide to speeding up a slow website — and it also makes images cheaper to store, quicker to share and kinder to visitors on mobile data.

Key takeaways

  • Resize first, format second, quality third — in that order.
  • 75–85% quality is visually lossless for most photographs.
  • Always keep your original as the master and compress a copy.

Frequently asked questions

Does compressing an image always reduce quality?

Lossy formats discard some data, but at sensible settings the difference is invisible in normal use. Lossless compression (PNG) keeps every pixel but produces much larger files.

What is the best format for website images?

WebP for most photos and graphics, with JPEG as a fallback for older software. PNG is for graphics that need transparency or pixel-perfect edges.

How small should a web image be?

As a guide: under 200 KB for in-page images and under 500 KB for large hero images is a healthy target most sites can hit.
