Bookkeeping Basics Every Small Business Should Know

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

Bookkeeping basics are the difference between a business that knows its numbers and one that guesses until tax season. Bookkeeping simply means recording every amount that comes in and goes out, in an organised way, while it is still fresh. This guide covers what to record, the cash-versus-profit trap, and a weekly routine small enough to actually keep.

The bookkeeping basics: what to record

  • Every sale — date, customer, amount, and whether it has been paid yet.
  • Every expense — date, supplier, what it was for, amount, and the receipt or invoice as proof.
  • Money owed to you and by you — unpaid invoices in both directions.
  • Tax collected — sales tax or VAT on sales is never your money; track it separately from income.

Keep business and personal money in separate accounts from day one. Mixing them is the single most common bookkeeping mess, and untangling a year of mixed statements costs far more than the second account ever would.

Cash is not profit

The most dangerous misunderstanding in small business: money in the bank feels like profit. It is not. Profit is sales minus all costs — including stock bought but unsold, tax that will be due, and invoices you have issued but not yet been paid for. A business can be profitable and cash-poor (waiting on late payers) or cash-rich and unprofitable (spending next month's tax). Your records are how you see both truths at once.

A weekly bookkeeping routine that sticks

  • 30 minutes, same time weekly: enter the week's sales and expenses, file the receipts (a photo is fine).
  • Chase overdue invoices — politely, promptly; late payment is a cash-flow killer (our invoice guide covers wording that gets paid faster).
  • Reconcile the bank account — match records to the statement so nothing is missed or doubled.
  • Monthly: set aside a percentage of income for tax in a separate account, and glance at the big picture — what sold, what cost, what is owed.

Spreadsheets or software?

A tidy spreadsheet is fine at very low volume; dedicated bookkeeping software earns its cost quickly by importing bank transactions, generating invoices and preparing tax figures. Whichever you choose, consistency beats sophistication — the same simple system used weekly beats advanced software opened twice a year. Knowing your numbers also strengthens everything else: pricing (our pricing guide), loan applications and honest rate-setting.

Key takeaways

  • Record sales, expenses, debts and tax — weekly, while it is fresh.
  • Separate accounts from day one; cash in the bank is not profit.
  • Reconcile monthly and set tax money aside as you earn.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I keep records?

Typically at least 5–7 years for tax purposes, depending on your country. Digital copies are acceptable in most systems — keep backups.

Do I need an accountant?

Not for day-to-day bookkeeping, but a good accountant at year-end (and when you start) usually saves more than they cost. They cannot help, though, if the underlying records are missing.

What is double-entry bookkeeping?

A system where every transaction is recorded in two places (for example, cash down, stock up), which makes errors easier to spot. Software does this invisibly; you just enter transactions normally.

Can I do bookkeeping myself?

Yes — most small businesses do. The skill is the habit, not the maths.
