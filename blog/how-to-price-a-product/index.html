How to Price a Product: Costs, Margin and Markup Explained

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

Knowing how to price a product is a survival skill: price too high and nobody buys; price too low and every sale quietly loses money. Good pricing starts from your true costs, adds a margin your business can live on, and then checks itself against the market. This guide walks through each step — including the margin-versus-markup confusion that trips up experienced sellers.

Step 1: Count the full cost of the product

Your cost is more than the purchase or materials price. Add packaging, shipping to you, platform and payment fees, returns allowance, and a share of your time and overheads (storage, software, stall or listing fees). Sellers who price from the materials cost alone are donating the rest. Our Unit Price Calculator helps compare supplier offers fairly when pack sizes differ.

Step 2: Margin and markup — not the same thing

  • Markup is added on top of cost: cost $40, markup 50% → price $60.
  • Margin is the share of the final price that is profit: price $60, cost $40 → profit $20, which is a 33% margin.

Confusing the two is expensive: a seller who wants a 50% margin but applies a 50% markup actually earns 33% — on thin margins, that gap is the whole business. Use our Profit Margin Calculator to convert between cost, price, margin and markup without the headache.

Step 3: Sanity-check against the market

Now compare with competitors. If your cost-based price is far above the market, the answer is rarely “charge it anyway” — it is to cut costs, differentiate the product (quality, service, story, speed), or choose a different product. If you are far below the market, you may be leaving money on the table — or sitting on a genuine advantage worth advertising. Also plan for discounts before you need them: a price that only works at full price breaks the first time you run a sale, so check sale scenarios with the Discount Calculator.

Do not forget the break-even check

Your price must also cover your fixed costs at a realistic sales volume. Our break-even guide shows how price, costs and volume fit together — the final check before any price goes public.

Key takeaways

  • Price from full cost — materials, fees, packaging, time and overheads.
  • Margin is profit as a share of price; markup is added to cost. Never confuse them.
  • Check the price against the market and against your break-even point.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good profit margin?

It varies hugely by industry — grocery margins are thin, digital products are fat. Compare within your own sector, and judge your margin after all costs, not before.

Should I match competitors' prices?

Only if your costs let you. Matching a price below your break-even is volunteering for losses; compete on value where you cannot compete on cost.

How often should I review prices?

Whenever supplier costs, fees or shipping change — and at least a few times a year. Small, regular increases beat rare, shocking ones.

Does a lower price always sell more?

Not always. Price signals quality; too cheap can reduce trust. Test changes on part of your range and watch real results.
