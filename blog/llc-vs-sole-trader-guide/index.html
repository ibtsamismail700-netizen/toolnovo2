LLC vs Sole Trader: A Plain-English Starting Guide

⚠ Please note

General information only — not financial advice. Figures in this article are examples to explain how the maths works. Your own rates, fees and terms will differ, so confirm everything with your lender, insurer or a licensed adviser before you decide.

LLC vs sole trader (in the UK, a limited company vs sole trader) is the first structural choice most founders meet — usually before they have earned anything. The two options differ in liability, paperwork, cost and how tax works. This is a plain-English overview of those differences in general terms; it is not legal or tax advice, and the right answer depends on your country, income and risk.

Sole trader: you are the business

As a sole trader (called a sole proprietor in the US), there is no legal separation between you and the business. Starting is simple and cheap — often just registering for tax — paperwork is light, and profits are taxed as your personal income. The trade-off is unlimited liability: if the business owes money or is sued, your personal assets are exposed. For low-risk, low-cost starts, this simplicity is exactly why most people begin here.

LLC / limited company: the business is its own person

An LLC (US) or limited company (UK) is a separate legal entity. It can own assets, sign contracts and carry debts in its own name, and the owners' liability is generally limited to what they put in — personal assets are usually protected if the business fails. The costs: formation fees, more formal accounting, separate business tax filings, and rules about how you take money out (salary, dividends, or both). Lenders and landlords may still ask owners for personal guarantees, which quietly reintroduces personal risk — read them carefully (see how business loans work).

LLC vs sole trader: the differences that matter

  • Liability — sole trader: personal and unlimited. Company: limited, with exceptions (fraud, guarantees, some taxes).
  • Paperwork — sole trader: minimal. Company: annual accounts, filings, stricter record rules.
  • Tax shape — sole trader: profit taxed as personal income. Company: the company is taxed, then you are taxed on what you extract — sometimes more efficient at higher profits, sometimes not; it depends on current rates and your other income.
  • Credibility — some clients and suppliers prefer or require a company; others do not care at all.
  • Privacy — company details (directors, accounts summaries) are publicly filed in many countries; sole trader details are not.

A common path

Many founders start as sole traders while income is small and risk is low, then incorporate when profits grow, contracts demand it, or liability becomes a real concern. Changing later is normal and usually straightforward — though assets, contracts and tax registrations must move properly, which is a job for an accountant's checklist. Whichever structure you choose, the foundations are the same: clean bookkeeping and honest tax tracking from day one.

Key takeaways

  • Sole trader: simplest and cheapest, but liability is personal.
  • LLC/company: liability protection and credibility, in exchange for cost and paperwork.
  • Many businesses start simple and incorporate as they grow — get advice at that turning point.

Frequently asked questions

Which is cheaper to run?

Sole trader, almost always — fewer filings and simpler accounts. The company's extra cost buys liability protection and sometimes tax efficiency at higher profits.

Does an LLC stop me being sued personally?

It protects you from most business debts and claims, but not from your own wrongdoing, personal guarantees you sign, or certain unpaid taxes. Insurance still matters.

Can I change from sole trader to a company later?

Yes — it is a well-trodden path. You form the company, transfer assets and contracts, register it for taxes and close the sole-trader registration appropriately.

Which do clients prefer?

Larger organisations sometimes require suppliers to be companies; small clients rarely mind. Ask your market if you are unsure.
